A shopping day built on a history lesson

On the second Saturday of September, Downtown Watseka handed out passports and asked people to walk. Twenty shops, twenty stamps, and a two-hundred-year-old trade route running right down the middle of it.

Hubbard Trail Days ran from 11 AM to 5 PM on Saturday, September 12, 2026. Passports came from the Santa House in the First Trust parking lot, or straight from a phone, and every participating business along the way had a stamp waiting at the counter.

Tables came out onto the sidewalk and stayed there all day. There were flowers in buckets on Walnut Street, chalk on the pavement, a prize wheel outside Harbor House, popcorn, snow cones, free popsicles from W.G.F.A., and a fire truck the kids were allowed to climb. Shops that are usually quiet on a Saturday afternoon had lines at the register.

Stamped passports came back to the Santa House, where volunteers counted them and entered people into the drawings on the spot: ten stamps, fifteen stamps, or the full twenty. The drawings were held in the days that followed and winners were contacted directly.

None of it was ticketed and none of it was gated. It was a free day downtown, paid for by the businesses and neighbours listed further down this page.

Mile Markers

Our supporters and donors. Hubbard Trail Days cost visitors nothing, which only works because these businesses, organisations and neighbours paid for the printing, the prizes and the extras that filled the street.

And every stamp stop. All twenty participating businesses opened their doors, kept a stamp at the counter and handled a day of foot traffic, free of charge. They are listed further down this page. Thank you as well to the volunteers who staffed the Santa House, counted stamps and ran the Kids Zone, and to Perfect Pawz for the dog treats and bandanas.

How the trail worked

Paper or phone, it counted the same. Here is the shape of the day, kept on the page for anyone planning to walk it with us next time.

1

Pick up a passport

Paper passports were handed out at the Santa House in the First Trust parking lot. A digital passport did the same job from a phone.

2

Collect the stamps

Walkers stepped into each participating shop, looked around, and asked for the stamp. Twenty stops, twenty different stamps.

3

Turn it in

Stamped passports came back to the Santa House. A volunteer counted them and entered each walker in the drawing they had earned.

Why “Hubbard”?

Two hundred years ago a young fur trader named Gurdon Hubbard set up a trading post where the Iroquois River meets Sugar Creek, the ground Downtown Watseka stands on today. The Potawatomi called him Pa-pa-ma-ta-be, or Swift-Walker, after he covered seventy-five miles on foot through the night to warn settlers of a threatened raid, arriving before dawn.

The path he wore north toward Chicago became known as Hubbard’s Trace. Settlers followed it, it widened into a road, and that road is now Illinois Route 1, still running straight through our downtown. Every storefront here sits on a two-hundred-year-old trade route.

But our town does not carry his name. It carries hers. Watchekee, “Daughter of the Evening Star,” was a Potawatomi woman and the daughter of the respected leader Shabbona. She married Hubbard here in 1826. After the 1833 Treaty of Chicago forced her people west, she walked back to this land again and again, thousands of miles over a lifetime of removals. In 1865, Middleport was renamed Watseka in her honor.

Most people here, visitors and lifelong residents alike, had never heard that story. On September 12 they collected it one stamp at a time.
